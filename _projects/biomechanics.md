This project was a two-week-long research project that I completed as part of the Marine Biomechanics course at Friday Harbor Laboratories in Washington. During this project, I studied the biomechanics of oysters. Specifically, I explored how shelf-like extrusions on the shell, called flutes, influence the hydrodynamics and temperature regulation of oysters.

Flutes are illustrated in the figure below. Interestingly, oysters of the same species (here, the Pacific Oyster) may or may not exhibit fluting.

I conducted a few different types of experiments during this project. First, I studied the heat transfer of Pacific oysters in a small wind tunnel. I hypothesized that fluted oysters stay cooler than smooth oysters for two possible reasons: 

(1) At low wind speeds, flutes can promote convective cooling by generating more boundary layer turbulence.
(2) Fluted shells can retain more water in their crevices and prolong the evaporative cooling process.

To explore (1), I measured the shell temperature of fluted and smooth oysters at 5 different wind speeds: 0 m/s, 0.7 m/s, 1.2 m/s, 2.5 m/s, and 3.8 m/s. The same experimental setup was used to study (2), but in these trials, there was no wind, only heat from the light source above the wind tunnel. I found that there was no significant difference in shell temperature between fluted and smooth oysters for any of the wind speed treatments. However, I did find that there was a significant difference in internal oyster temperature during the no wind trials. Results from both sets of experiments are shown below.
